The place Kaia picked was two streets off the main road. A cafe under a HDB block, with air conditioning. Six tables, a chalkboard menu, a woman at the pass who plated everything herself. Wen had walked past it a dozen times without going in.

"You choose the place with actual effort," Kaia said, sliding into the seat across from her. She stretched, arms out over her head. Her hair was still slightly damp at the ends. "Slept until three-ish. Showered. This is technically my morning."

"It's eight."

"I know. Doesn't feel like it." She picked up the menu without opening it, set it down again. "I'll have whatever has the most butter in it."

They ordered. Wen ordered the one with the basil and cheese. Kaia ordered a carbonara with a side of salad.

The bread came first, then water, then the particular quiet of two people who had already done the easy part of getting to know each other and were deciding, without saying so, whether to do the harder part tonight.

"I want to tell you something," Wen said. "And I don't want you to make it a big thing."

"I can't promise that. But I'll try to make it a medium thing."

Wen turned her water glass a quarter turn. "Han Qin. We're…. it's not bad. Two years. I'm not leaving him tonight, or this month, oh look, I don't know. I love him. There's no part of this where I tell you and it means I've already decided something."

"Okay."

"That's it? Okay?"

"You haven't told me the thing yet." Kaia tore a piece of bread, didn't eat it. "You told me the frame around the thing."

Wen almost laughed. The tightness in her chest loosening half a degree at being caught so plainly, so early, without punishment in it. "Right."

She told her about the carousel. Sunday morning, six forty-three, the word landing like something she'd swallowed without chewing. Kaia told her about the book, said she should read it and she did. She lay in bed for a long while after, staring at the ceiling.

"I thought it was just me," Wen said. "Like something that's wrong with me. The wiring I was born with, or maybe even, something I'd trained myself out of feeling because it was easier not to want anything from anyone for a while. I didn't know it was a… a category. That other people had already named it and were walking around with it and it wasn't a problem they were solving. It was just what they were."

Kaia didn't say anything for a second. Wen noticed the way Kaia's stillness before speaking was different.

"Huh. That's the first time you've said that out loud," Kaia said.

"Yes."

"How does it feel."

Wen considered it honestly, the way the question deserved. "Lighter than I thought it would and heavier than I wanted it to be. Both."

"That tracks."

{% include divider.html %}

The pasta arrived. Kaia twirled hers, ate two forkfuls before she spoke again, unhurried in the way that had nothing to do with rudeness and everything to do with not needing to fill a silence just because it existed.

"I've dated men and women," she said. "You should know that about me, since we're doing the honest dinner. My last one was with a man. There was a lot of friction. He was sweet at first. He pushed me in ways that showed that the difference between us couldn't be bridged. How I worked. The friends I had. How I needed to live. What I needed to be supported. He wasn't a bad person. He just needed me to be someone else."

"What happened?"

"It took me longer than it should have to see it clearly. That's usually how it goes. You're inside the thing, you can't see the whole shape of it, you need distance." She ate another forkful. "I've experimented with different relationship structures too. Kink. ENM… Hey, I'm not saying that to shock you. I'm saying I know the landscape from the inside, not from a book someone gave me."

"Kaia, you gave me the book."

"I know. I picked it because it was closest to true, not because it would fix anything." A small shrug. "Books don't fix anything. They just make the shape you already are easier to point at."

Wen turned that over. "So what do I do."

"You ask yourself what you want. What you actually want. Not what our culture says is allowed. The messy kind. Not what your mother can easily tell her friends. And you go from there." Kaia said it plainly, no weight put under it to make it sound like wisdom. "That's what I did. Not as a plan. I am not going to lie to you, it felt brutal. As a question I kept asking until the answer stopped changing."

"That's not an answer."

"It's not supposed to be an answer. It's just my way." Kaia looked at her. "The last relationship wasn't just him being wrong for me. I was learning things about what I wanted too, what I needed, what I could actually say out loud, and what I'd been silent about because saying it felt like too much work. I wasn't just enduring him. I was collecting information."

{% include divider.html %}

"I wrote something," Wen said. "Published it, actually. Last night."

"The one from that Tuesday. The night you wrote three lines and crossed out the rest."

Wen hadn't expected her to remember the image that precisely. "That one. It turned into an essay. About my parents, mostly, the arithmetic of what it cost them to give me the life they thought I was supposed to want."

"I read it. Three in the morning, wasn't it."

"I didn't tell you it was up."

"You didn't need to." Kaia said it without making a thing of it. "It's not hard to find someone's Substack once you know the handle." A small shrug. "The twelve dollars. I thought about it the whole shift after."

"What did you think?"

"That it wasn't a metaphor. It was a real number." She turned her glass a half-turn. "People will read it and think it's about meaning, and it is, but you also just did the maths in public. That's a harder thing to do than the meaning part."

Wen didn't have anything ready for that, so she ate instead.

"I did more maths after," she said, eventually. "Not my parents'. Mine."

Kaia waited, the way she waited for things that mattered, no prompting and just room.

"If I left the flat, Han Qin's flat, I worked out what it would actually take. Eight days of consultancy work a month covers it. Floor, buffer, saving. I've been doing twenty-two." She said it plainly, the way she'd learned to say things at this table, without building the case first. "I'm not leaving. I want to be clear about that again. But I know now that I could. That's different from not knowing."

"It's very different."

"I don't know what to do with knowing it."

Kaia considered this with the same unhurried seriousness she gave everything, not inflating it, not talking her down from it either. "You don't have to make a big move," she said. "Nobody's handing you a script where the next scene is quitting the flat and the relationship and the whole life, all in one night, dramatic music. You start small. You find the one true thing you can do this week, and you do that. Then you find the next one."

"What's the one thing."

"I don't know. You do." A pause, not unkind. "But it's probably smaller than you think it needs to be."

{% include divider.html %}

"Work's been a lot," Kaia said, later, after the plates had thinned and the woman at the pass had come by once to ask if everything was good and gone again satisfied. "Not the patients. The patients are the good part. The rest of it."

"The politics."

"The admin, the metrics, and everyone above bay level deciding what the work should look like from a spreadsheet. I don't have the energy to fight all of it. I want to do the part I love and let the rest happen around me as quietly as possible." She turned her glass. "It doesn't come home with me, mostly. But it's there. Friction in the system, even when I'm not touching the system."

"Is there anyone at work who, I don't know, makes it easier?"

Kaia's mouth did something Wen couldn't quite name, not quite a smile and something more private than that. "There's someone. Met her a few months back, actually. Odd way. She was covering nights on my ward, which nobody in admin does. I clocked it fast. Asked her straight out if HR sent her to spy on me."

"Did they?"

"She didn't say no." Kaia picked up her water, drank, set it down. "I asked her outright and she didn't lie to me. She said, 'that's the most honest thing I've said on this ward in three nights.'" A small laugh, more air than sound. "That's it. That's the whole reason. She could have said something soft and I'd have let it go and probably never trusted her. She didn't. She let me sit with the actual shape of it."

Wen thought of her own mother, the barley water, the way love in her house arrived sideways, never named directly. "That's a strange thing to build trust on."

"It's the only thing I build trust on." Kaia shrugged. "She's been, I don't know what to call it. Watching out for me. Not in a way that makes me a project. She's the one who suggested I look at training further. Not the promotion track. Nobody wants me managing rosters. A different lane. Teaching."

"Teaching what?"

"Birth educator." Kaia said it like she'd been waiting for someone to ask properly. "Working with couples before the crisis point. Not instead of the hospital. Before it. So when they get to us they're not walking in blank, terrified, handing over the whole thing because nobody told them there was a choice to make. I want people to arrive already knowing what they can ask for." She turned her fork over, set it down. "That's the actual thing. Not the ward. Upstream of the ward."

Wen watched something in Kaia's face she hadn't seen before. Not the composure, not the dry economy of her usual sentences. Something closer to want, undisguised.

"You should do it," Wen said.

"I'm doing it. Slowly. There's a course. I've looked at the dates twice and not signed up yet."

"Why not?"

Kaia considered this with the same unhurried honesty she gave everything. "Because it's the first thing I've wanted this specifically in a long time, and wanting things specifically means you can lose them specifically." She picked the fork back up. "I'll sign up. I'm telling you now so it's real. You're a witness."

"I'm honoured."

"You should be. I don't make things real out loud very often."

Somewhere in the space between what they were saying and what neither of them added to it, the other thing sat quietly at the table. What Wen had said weeks ago at the workshop, *I think I like you*, hadn't gone anywhere since. Kaia hadn't taken it back. She also hadn't done anything with it. It stayed where it had stayed every time since: acknowledged, unmanaged, neither of them in a hurry to close the door on it or walk through it while Han Qin still had a key to Wen's evenings. Tonight it was simply support. For the maths, for the piece, and for the not-yet-decided. And support, for now, was enough shape for it to take.

They split what was left of the bread. Outside, the street had gone quiet in the particular way Singapore streets went quiet on a Saturday close to ten. Not empty, just settled, the day's noise folded away somewhere.

"Actually," Wen said. "You should get a reading. There's a woman. She runs a café near the hospital and does astrology on the side. I recognised her years ago from somewhere and could never place it until I saw her doing TikTok lives with bags. I don't know how to describe her. She's compressed. Like she says four words and you spend a week understanding them."

"A reading."

"She'd probably see straight through you in about ninety seconds."

Kaia filed it the way Wen had come to recognise she filed most things. Not agreeing, not dismissing, just setting it somewhere she could find again later. "Maybe."

"You don't have to."

"No, I like it. I like people who see straight through things. Saves time." She wiped her hands, looked at Wen properly. "You're doing okay. With all of it."

"I don't know yet what all of it even is."

"That's still an answer to the question I asked." Kaia signalled for the bill. "You're not running from him. You're not building a case against him so you can leave without it being your fault. You're just finding out what shape you actually are. That's a different project entirely. Slower. Less dramatic. Probably better for everyone in the end."

Wen didn't say anything. It was, she thought, exactly true, and she hadn't had the words for it until Kaia handed them to her, plain, across a small table, over a plate that had had too much butter in it.

They split the bill without discussing it, the kind of thing that had stopped needing discussion somewhere in the last few weeks.

Outside, on the pavement, Kaia touched her arm once, briefly, before turning toward the MRT.

"Text me when you land wherever you're landing," she said.

"I don't know when that'll be."

"I know. Text me anyway."
