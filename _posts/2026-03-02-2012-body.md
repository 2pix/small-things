The room looked good.

Light falling long across the table, the kind that arrived in the late morning and stayed unhurried. Screens open. Two of them — Priya and James — arguing over typography like the difference between serif and sans was a moral position, voices low and certain. Someone laughing at something half-heard from the back. Coffee cooling slowly beside a stack of mockups, the surface of it gone still, a thin skin forming at the edge of the cup that no one would drink.

He stood at the doorway for a moment and let himself watch.

This was his.

A small advertising studio. Built slowly, the way things built on actual ground are built — one decision at a time, one person chosen, one client earned. People he'd wanted here. People who had, in their own time, wanted to be here back. That reciprocity had felt important to him, more important than the portfolio, more important than the billings. He had turned down people who were brilliant but restless. He had kept people who were steadier and less spectacular and deeply reliable, the kind who arrived before they were asked and stayed until the thing was right.

They were good.

Not flashy. Not the kind of good that needed an audience. Just the quiet, grinding, accumulating kind — delivering, learning, growing sharper in ways that didn't announce themselves until suddenly a client noticed something they couldn't name, a quality that had been building in the work for months.

He had widened the scope over the past year. Taken on projects he wouldn't have touched before, work that required more range, more coordination, more of him present in rooms he hadn't previously needed to be in. Added complexity. Added revenue. Added responsibility that settled across his shoulders in a way that felt, at first, like weight that belonged there. The natural density of a thing becoming what it was always becoming.

Not because he wanted scale for its own sake.

Because he wanted continuity. Because there were people now whose housing loans were connected to his decisions, whose children's school fees moved in relation to whether he held the client relationships carefully enough, whether the invoices went out on time, whether he caught the problems early. He had become careful with numbers. Careful with contracts. Careful with the particular arithmetic of expansion — how much could be added before the thing that made it good started to thin.

Every new client meant stability for someone else.

He told himself this often. It was true. He also noticed, somewhere below the level of thought, that he had stopped asking what it meant for him.

The room looked beautiful. He felt proud of it in a way that didn't require anyone to confirm it, a warm and private thing, the satisfaction of having built something that worked and watching it work.

He walked to his desk. Sat. Opened the laptop.

The tiredness arrived the way it always did now — without drama, without the decency of announcing itself as something worth attending to. Not emotional tiredness, not the clean exhaustion of a hard day that sleep could return. Just weight. A low, constant pressure behind his eyes that was there when he woke and there when he closed them. A tightness across his chest that didn't belong to panic or grief, nothing so legible as that. A heaviness in his limbs that had stopped responding to rest the way it used to — sleep arriving, weight lifting, morning beginning clear.

He adjusted his chair.

Answered emails. Delegated. Approved revisions. Called a client back who'd left a message the evening before, his voice easy and warm and utterly professional, the kind of presence he'd learned to generate from inside the fatigue, wearing it lightly enough that no one noticed the effort underneath.

The team moved easily around him. They always had. That ease had been one of the things he was proudest of — a studio where people didn't work around the founder's moods, didn't calibrate their energy to his, didn't shrink when he walked through a room. He had worked hard for that atmosphere, the ordinary freedom of it. Watched other agencies contort themselves around founders who needed managing and felt something close to relief that he had built something different.

He kept widening.

New projects. Larger budgets. More oversight, more coordination, more of himself distributed across more relationships, more deliverables, more people who needed something from him by end of day.

Each addition felt responsible. Each addition had a reason. Each addition had a spreadsheet line that justified it, a relationship it deepened, a team member it gave more hours to.

His body did not argue.

It complied.

The way a very well-trained thing complies — not from willingness exactly, but from the deep habit of compliance, the long practice of continuing. He had asked it to hold weight and it had held weight and he had asked it to hold more and it had continued, adjusting, accommodating, finding ways to carry what was added without making a scene about it.

Until it didn't.

The first refusal was small enough that he almost didn't register it as refusal. He sat with a proposal open on his screen — a mid-sized project, a client he knew, nothing unusual — and stared at it. The words were there. The logic was clear. His mind knew what the proposal needed. But somewhere between knowing and doing, something had stepped back. A quiet blankness where the reach for energy should have been, like putting your hand out in the dark and finding the shelf has moved.

He stood to get water.

His legs felt less certain under him than they should have. Not pain. Not weakness in any measurable sense. Just a slight softening of their authority, as if the signal from his brain was arriving a half-beat late, as if his body was processing the instruction with a small, dignified hesitation.

He got the water.

He came back.

No collapse. No scene. Just the sense that something inside him had stepped back half a pace and was watching from slightly further away, curious about how long this would continue.

The studio kept running. Clients kept calling. Invoices kept clearing. He kept answering.

At night the tiredness deepened in a way that felt less like the end of a day and more like submersion, the slow heavy going-under of a thing that had been in water too long. Sleep came quickly — his body grateful for the surrender — but did not restore. He woke with the same weight he had closed his eyes carrying, as if the night had offered him eight hours of horizontal and nothing more.

He told himself this was normal.

Growth costs. Responsibility costs. Leadership costs. He had read enough, talked to enough people who had built things, to know that this was part of the shape of it.

He began reading at night. Articles from the US, forums, biohacking threads. Creatives talking about staying sharp. Staying ahead. The language was clean and confident — cognitive enhancement, stack protocols, performance optimization. Men who ran companies and recorded podcasts about their morning routines and seemed to have solved, or nearly solved, the problem of the body's inconvenient limits.

L-theanine. Modafinil. Racetams. Adaptogens. Phosphatidylserine. Alpha-GPC. Lion's mane extract arriving in amber glass.

He ordered a few things quietly. Small capsules in matte bottles, arriving in plain packaging, stacked on his desk beside the laptop. Nothing dramatic. Nothing reckless. Just edge. The kind of edge that kept the width manageable. That kept the room full of people who trusted him to hold it together.

He told himself it was optimization.

He did not ask what he was optimizing away from.

The numbers still worked.

The team still smiled.

The room still looked beautiful in the late morning light.

His body stopped negotiating.

One morning he arrived before anyone else. He did this sometimes — came in early to have the studio to himself for an hour, to move through it quietly, to feel it belong to him before the day filled it with other people's needs. He made coffee. He sat at his desk. He opened the laptop and looked at the screen and felt the hollow widen, slow and certain, from somewhere below his ribs.

Not anxiety. Not the productive tightness of a hard problem.

Not sadness, which at least has texture, which at least arrives with its own logic.

Absence.

As if something essential had quietly gathered itself and stepped out of the room. Not dramatically. Not with accusation. Simply departed.

He closed the laptop.

Got up. Went to the kitchen. Filled a glass.

He stood at the counter and opened the matte bottle. Tapped out the capsules into his palm. Four. Then a fifth. He looked at them for a moment — the modest pile of them, their careful colors, their promise of edge and clarity and the particular alertness that felt, if you didn't examine it too closely, like being okay.

He swallowed them with the water.

Rinsed the glass. Set it down.

Outside, the street was beginning to come alive. Somewhere below, the elevator clicked. The studio would fill in twenty minutes. There were calls this morning, a presentation this afternoon, a conversation he needed to have with James about the pitch they'd lost and how to move forward.

His hands rested on the counter.

He stood there in the early quiet, the coffee cooling behind him, the room still and waiting.

Something in him was waiting too. He couldn't have said what.
