This is part of The Black Cat — a novel in pieces.

The plane came in low over open water and David watched the coastline arrange itself below. Flat. Dense. The particular geometry of a city that had decided what it was going to be and built accordingly.

He had a window seat. He'd asked for one specifically, which he didn't usually do.

His bag was in the overhead locker. Light. He'd left things in Chiang Mai that he hadn't needed to bring back. Not deliberately. Just found, packing, that certain things had stopped being his in the two weeks he'd been away.

He had his notebook open but hadn't written in it.

He was making a list in his head instead.

Not of things to do. Of conversations.

The conversations arrived in the right order without him having to arrange them. His crew first. Then the clients. Then the agencies who might take on the accounts. Then Karen.

He noticed the order and didn't adjust it.

Outside, the coastline disappeared under cloud.

He closed the notebook.

Marina had left the light on in the kitchen.

He set his bag down in the entrance and stood for a moment in the particular quiet of a home that knows how to hold people without requiring anything from them. The smell of it. Twenty years of meals and clean laundry and the specific patience of someone who showed up every day without needing the showing up to mean more than it did.

He left his bag where it was and went to the kitchen.

There was food covered on the stove.

He ate standing up. Not from hunger exactly. From something that needed the simple fact of a meal prepared by someone who knew he was coming.

He washed the plate when he finished.

Then he went to his old room.

He stood in it for a moment before turning on the light. The desk where he had done homework. The shelf with the things he'd left when he moved out and never come back for. The window that faced the neighbour's wall. The particular smallness of it — not uncomfortable. Just true.

He sat at the desk and opened his laptop.

The company account first. The number sat there without judgment. Enough. More than enough for what he needed to do.

The lease. Eleven months remaining. Manageable.

The contact list. Forty-three names. Clients, crew, agencies. He moved through them slowly, sorting without hurrying. The ones who could be handed off intact. The ones who would need transition. The ones he owed a direct conversation.

The crew were six. Three of them had been with him more than four years.

They had stopped being his the same way everything else had. Not badly. Just completely.

He had thought about what he owed them separately, on the plane. Full notice paid out. Three months beyond the legal minimum for the ones who had been there longest. Before the end of the week he would call — not email — every agency he trusted, and say what each person could do and what kind of room they worked best in. He knew this. He had watched them long enough to know.

No one would find out the studio was closing from a client, or from a forwarded message. Each person would hear it directly.

It wasn't generosity. It was how you closed something that had been real.

He moved the six names to a separate column and left them there.

At some point the city outside went quiet.

He kept working.

Not with urgency. With the particular patience of someone removing what doesn't belong. Seeing what remains.

When he stopped it was past midnight. He hadn't finished. He had done enough.

He slept.

He called his father from the kitchen the next morning, standing at the counter.

He was thinking of staying at home for a while, if that was alright.

His father said of course. The room is there.

They didn't go further than that.

He went back to his room and called Karen.

She picked up quickly.

"I'm back," he said.

A pause. Not surprise. Something else. "I know."

"I'd like to see you."

She didn't answer immediately. "When."

"Whenever works."

"Tomorrow," she said. "Morning." She named the place. He said yes.

She was there when he arrived.

She had chosen a table near the back, against the wall, with a clear sightline to the entrance. She had a coffee already. She looked up when he came through the door.

He got a coffee at the counter before sitting down.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey." She had both hands around the cup. Not gripping it. Just around it.

He sat.

"You look tired," she said.

"I slept fine."

She nodded once. She didn't ask what he meant by that.

He told her about the studio. All forty-three names. The agencies. The lease. She listened without interrupting, without filling in his sentences. When he finished she said, "When did you decide."

"I don't know. Before Chiang Mai. Maybe before Guangzhou." He paused. "Maybe in Guangzhou."

She was quiet. Her thumb moved once, slowly, around the side of the cup.

"Is there someone else?" She asked it the way she asked everything — evenly, without preamble. Her eyes stayed on his. "Since China. Something changed."

"No."

"Are you sure."

"Yes."

She held his gaze a moment longer. She was looking for something specific. She found what she needed and looked at the table.

"Then what," she said.

"I can't ignore it anymore."

She nodded slowly.

"What does that mean for us?" Her voice was steady. She was someone who stayed level.

"I don't know," he said. "I only know my part."

"And your part is?"

"No studio. Moving home." He paused. "I don't know yet what I want for work. I'm not going to ask you to wait for an answer I can't promise will arrive."

She was quiet for a long time.

The café made its sounds around them.

"It looked like you knew what you were doing," she said. "From the outside. It always did."

"It did from the inside too. For a long time." He stopped. "It doesn't anymore."

She set the cup down.

Her hands went to her lap.

"Do you love me?"

"Yes."

She exhaled slowly. Like putting something down carefully, not dropping it.

"I don't know if that's enough," she said.

He didn't say anything.
