The alarm went at five-forty.

Mark reached across and cancelled it before the second pulse. He dressed without turning the light on.

The kitchen at five-fifty had a scent. The air held the residue of yesterday's rush. The block was not yet awake — he could hear it in the silence, the absence of sounds that meant people moving through their mornings. A lone ding from a lift somewhere. A door. Nothing continuous.

He filled the kettle. Set it to boil. Stood at the counter while it did.

Through the kitchen window: the estate arranging itself in the early light. The covered walkways. A mynah on the railing opposite, doing nothing, going nowhere. The sky a deep royal blue, lit from behind — before the sun had any angle on it.

He thought about the brioche. He'd proofed the dough the night before — it would be ready to shape now, needed another hour in the tin before baking. He had to be at the café by eight. The timing was tight but it worked if he moved without hurrying.

He did not hurry.

He was shaping the loaves when Lina appeared.

She moved through the kitchen without greeting him — not rudeness. She filled a glass of water. Drank it standing at the sink.

She looked at what he was doing. The way his hands worked the dough — not kneading, shaping, the economy of someone who had learned a thing properly and now did it without thinking about it.

"Brioche again."

"It sells."

She made a sound that wasn't quite agreement. She had opinions about what the café sold, offered them when she had them, and did not repeat them. He had learned this about her.

She sat at the kitchen table with her phone and her reading glasses. He shaped.

{% include divider.html %}

After a while she said, without looking up: "I need to photograph the new one."

"The grey?"

"Pewter. It's different."

He glanced over. The bag was on the table beside her — a structured tote, the grey that was almost blue in some light, a handle that sat properly rather than drooping. She had been working on the hardware for three weeks. He knew this because she had mentioned it once and then not mentioned it again.

"The light's better in the corridor at this hour," he said.

"I know. I need someone to hold it."

He looked at the dough. Looked at the clock. "Give me ten minutes."

He finished shaping. Got the loaves into the tins. Set them to proof. Washed his hands.

"Okay."

{% include divider.html %}

The corridor outside the flat was east-facing — it caught the early light without the direct sun that would come later. Lina knew this — she had been photographing bags in this corridor for three years. The knowing hadn't made the process faster.

She propped the bag against the ledge. Stepped back. Looked at her phone.

"It's not sitting right."

He crouched and adjusted the handle. Stood back.

"Too much shadow on the side."

He moved it two inches.

She took three photos. Looked at them. "The handle."

He adjusted the handle.

She took three more. "Better. But the background."

The background was the corridor wall — a beige that photographed as something between beige and grey depending on the light.

"It's the same wall it always is," he said.

"It photographs differently today."

He did not argue with this.

They tried the handle against the wall, the bag on the floor with the light from above, the bag held at arm's length — which Lina did herself, extending it into the frame while he took the photo on her phone, which required him to find the angle that caught both the bag and the light without her arm appearing. This took four attempts.

She looked at the result.

"That one," she said.

He looked. It was good. The pewter came through. The handle sat. The light did what light was supposed to do.

"Send it to me," she said. "I need to write the listing."

He sent it. She sat back down at the kitchen table.

"You're going to be late," she said. She said it without concern. Just information.

He checked the clock. He was not going to be late. He had built in the corridor.

He went back to the kitchen and the brioche.

{% include divider.html %}

By seven he had the loaves in the oven and the timer set.

She watched him move through the end of it — mobile phone, keys, the bag with what he needed. A man at the end of a sequence he had made into muscle.

"The timer's set. Forty-five minutes."

"I know how to use the oven."

"The temperature drops if you open it before thirty."

She looked at him over her glasses. "I have been using that oven since before you moved in."

"I know." He picked up his bag. "Don't open it before thirty."

She looked back at her phone. He was not wrong about the temperature.

"Safe walk," she said.

He went.

{% include divider.html %}

She sat for a while after he'd gone. The flat settled into its own quiet — a burnt smell still in the air, the kind that sat in the block before the sun came up and hadn't yet changed. A space that had recently held someone and now held only the evidence of them. The shaped loaves in the oven. The kettle still faintly warm.

She had known him before all of this. He had shown up at her door with a bag and the look of someone who had put down a very heavy thing and didn't yet know what to do with their hands. The flat in Tanjong Pagar settled, the father gone, the boyfriend already a chapter that had closed without a proper ending. He had not said much. She had not asked much. She had shown him the room and told him where the spare key was.

She knew what he had come from. The two years of it — the helpers, the hospital runs, the loneliness of being the only one in the room who understood the full shape of the situation. He had not had anyone to hand anything to. She knew this kind of carrying. She had done her own version, at different points, with different weight.

What she had not told him, because he had not been ready to hear it, was that the loneliness he arrived with was not only about his father. She had seen it in the chart the first time she read him, years ago at the workshop where they'd met. A man who had arranged his life around contact and arrived at isolation. Not because he had failed to connect. Because the form of contact he had been given does not produce intimacy. Show up. Handle it. Cost nothing. He had done everything right by the code. The code was the problem.

Not unusual. Just expensive, in the way things are expensive when you pay in instalments and don't notice the total.

He was cheaper to run now. That was the way she thought about it. Less maintenance on the inside. The café had done some of that — Mr Tan's kitchen first, then his own. Repetition as remedy. She had seen this before. The body finding its ground through the same motion, again and again, until the ground was actually there.

She picked up her phone. The listing to write. The pewter bag sitting on the table, waiting to become something someone else would buy and carry.

She wrote: Structured tote. Pewter. The handle sits.

She looked at it.

Added: Handmade. One of a kind.

That was enough.

The timer went at seven forty-five. She got up. Opened the oven — not before thirty. Took the loaves out. Set them to cool.

The estate at seven-fifteen had its own grammar. The uncles on the void deck, one of them digging his nose without looking up. A woman in scrubs moving quickly toward the bus stop. Two children in school uniform being walked by a helper, the children talking, the helper listening with most of her attention while some small part tracked the distance still to cover.

Mark walked through it without headphones.

He had done this every morning for two years. The walk from Spottiswoode to the café — twelve minutes direct, seventeen through the market. He usually went through the market. Because the market at this hour was the city being honest about what it was: the actual work of feeding people, done without decoration, by people who had been up since four.

He stopped at the usual stall. Kopi. The uncle knew the order. The transaction took forty-five seconds.

He walked.

The brioche. The day's list, which was short. Whether the new roast was right — he had been dialling it in for three weeks, close but not there.

He thought, briefly, about his father.

The helper with the two children — something in the way she was listening while tracking the distance, two things running simultaneously. He had learned to do that in those two years. He had not known it was something you could learn.

He turned it over once, the way you turn something over to check the underside, and then he let it go.

The café was three minutes away.

He finished the kopi.

He went in.
