This is part of The Black Cat — a novel in pieces.

He confirmed with the client at nine PM from his hotel room.

Numbers work. Second supplier visit tomorrow for due diligence. Back to you by end of week.

He put the phone down and looked at the ceiling.

The room was adequate. The kind of hotel that served business travelers who weren't being expensed generously — clean, functional, a window that looked onto another building, the sound of the city doing what it did at night, which was not stop. He had stayed in worse. He had stayed in better. The room had no opinion about either.

He opened his laptop. The return flight was there, the cursor hovering over the booking. Tuesday morning. Back in the office by afternoon. Two meetings he'd need to reschedule if he delayed.

He closed the laptop.

He told himself it was the second supplier. That confirming backup capacity was not optional. That a professional didn't leave a sourcing trip with one vendor and call it done. He told himself this clearly and with conviction and it was true enough that he couldn't argue with it.

He ordered room service. Ate without tasting much. Slept before he expected to.

The second factory was in Dongguan.

An hour by taxi through the same wide roads, the same construction, the same brown-white sky sitting over everything. This factory was newer. The floors cleaner. The lighting slightly less honest — someone had tried to make it look like something, had added a reception area with a company logo on the wall and a woman at a desk who smiled with the particular calibration of someone who had been told to smile at visitors.

David noticed the difference immediately.

The floor manager was younger than Mr Chen. Better English. He used phrases like quality assurance protocol and international compliance standards and walked David through the line with the energy of someone performing competence rather than having it.

The numbers were comparable. The certifications were in order. The timeline was achievable.

David asked his questions. Took the folder. Shook hands.

In the taxi back he looked at both folders side by side.

Technically equivalent.

He already knew which one he would recommend.

He sent the client a brief note. Second supplier assessed. Will confirm recommendation by Thursday.

Thursday was two days away.

He put the phone in his pocket.

Wednesday he didn't have anywhere to be.

He stood at the hotel entrance at eight in the morning with this fact and felt the particular quality of unstructured time — its weight, its slight vertigo, the way his mind immediately began generating tasks to fill it. He had emails. He had a proposal that needed revising. He had three things on a list that had been migrating from week to week for months, always necessary, never urgent enough.

He started walking instead.

Not toward anything. Just away from the hotel, into the streets, following the logic of what appeared in front of him. The industrial district at this hour was already running — roller doors open, the sound of machinery, the smell of the day's production beginning. Workers on motorcycles. A breakfast stall with steam rising from something in a pot, four plastic stools occupied by men eating in silence, the particular focused silence of people who have somewhere to be in thirty minutes.

He stopped and pointed at what the man next to him was eating.

The woman behind the stall served him without ceremony.

He ate standing up. Noodles, something braised, an egg. The broth had been going since before he woke up, the depth of it, the way it tasted like time spent on it. He paid what she indicated with her fingers. Less than two Singapore dollars.

He kept walking.

The city moved around him with its own logic and he had no brief, no folder, no client, no role inside it. He was just a body moving through space. His phone stayed in his pocket. His laptop was in the hotel room. The proposal could wait until Thursday. The emails could wait until Thursday.

His mind tried to run the numbers.

He let it run them. Watched it do it. The way you watch a machine doing what it was built for, without needing to intervene. The numbers ran. The projections projected. The risk models modeled. And underneath all of that, quieter than all of that, his body walked.

Past a school with children arriving, the sound of them bright and ungoverned. Past a temple he didn't go into, incense drifting out through an open door. Past a market where a woman was arranging vegetables with the attention of someone arranging something that mattered, each bunch placed with a care that wasn't for display, just for order, her own order, the order that made sense to her hands.

He stopped and watched her for a moment.

She didn't notice him. Or didn't mind.

He walked on.

By midday he had been walking for three hours. His feet knew this before his mind did — a heaviness in his calves, the particular warmth of shoes that have been in use. He found a tea house on a corner. Old wooden furniture, the kind that had been old for a long time without anyone deciding to replace it. A television in the corner running something nobody was watching. Four old men at a table with a board game between them, the game apparently secondary to the conversation, or the conversation secondary to the companionship, or the companionship requiring neither conversation nor game but simply the being-there of people who had been coming here long enough that the chairs knew them.

He sat down.

A boy of about sixteen brought tea without being asked. The pot was heavy. The tea was the colour of rust and tasted like something that had been grown in actual ground.

David sat with it.

His chest was quiet.

Not absent — he could feel it, the faithful repetition of it, the valve opening and closing, the blood going where blood goes. Just quiet. Doing what it did without commentary.

He thought about the studio.

About Priya and James arguing over typography. About the proposal that needed revising. About the client who had sent him here, who would call on Friday wanting the recommendation, who needed to know the margins were good and the timeline was achievable and the backup supplier was confirmed.

All of it was still there. All of it would be waiting.

But here, in this tea house, on this wooden chair, with this rust-coloured tea and these four old men whose game was secondary to their presence, he was not being required to widen anything.

The boy refilled his cup without asking.

He sat for another hour.

Thursday morning he wrote the recommendation. Forty minutes. It was good work — clear, specific, giving the client what they needed to decide. He sent it at nine-fifteen and closed the laptop.

He had a flight booked for Friday morning.

He looked at the booking for a long time.

Then he picked up the hotel phone and called the front desk and asked about checkout.

He booked one more night.

He told himself he wanted to see the Pearl River.

This was not entirely untrue.

He spent the afternoon walking along the embankment, the river wide and brown and moving with the patience of water that has been moving for a very long time and will continue after everything watching it is gone. Barges passed. The city on both banks continued its construction, its production, its perpetual becoming.

He stood at the railing and watched it.

His mind was quieter today. Not silent — it was never silent, he was beginning to understand that, beginning to accept the constant production of it the way you accept traffic noise when you live on a busy street. But quieter. The way it had been on the factory floor with Mr Chen. The way it had been in the tea house yesterday.

As if something in him had stopped pretending the noise was the point.

He stayed until the light changed. The particular hour when the brown-white sky went gold for twenty minutes before the dark came, the river holding the colour longer than anything else, the barges moving through it carrying their cargo to wherever cargo went.

He thought about the cable.

Not his client's batteries. The other kind. The one in his bag that had started fraying last week, the charge inconsistent now, sometimes working, sometimes not. He'd meant to replace it in Singapore. Hadn't.

He looked at the river.

He thought about a lot of things that didn't have names yet.

Then he went back to the hotel and packed his bag and in the morning he went to the airport and flew home.

The studio was as he'd left it.

The squeezing came back somewhere over the South China Sea.

He noticed it the way you notice something returning that you'd briefly forgotten — a faint surprise, then recognition, then the particular resignation of understanding that you haven't solved anything.

You've just been somewhere else for four days.

He looked out the window at the clouds below.

The factory floor. The tea house. The river at the hour when the light went gold.

He pressed his hand flat against his sternum.

Held it there.

The seat belt sign came on for landing.
