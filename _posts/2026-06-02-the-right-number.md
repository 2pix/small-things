This is part of The Black Cat — a novel in pieces.

I billed in six-minute increments. Every hour of the day was divisible, assignable, recoverable as income. Six minutes was the smallest unit of time the firm could convert into something countable. The number didn't lie. It said exactly what had been turned into something that would count.

My father had a stroke in August 2020. A Tuesday. He was at the Pinnacle. I was not.

He already had dementia. The stroke made it worse. COVID was also happening. The three of them arrived at the same time and did not negotiate with each other.

I learned to suction a tracheostomy from YouTube and from the first helper, who was patient with my questions and left within three months. The second stayed six weeks. The third stayed eight months. Long enough that I stopped instructing her and began consulting her. She left anyway. There was always a next person to train. The gap between one person leaving and the next arriving was a gap I filled. I took leave to do this. When leave ran out I took unpaid days. The firm was accommodating. Something had to give. It was never the billing.

The medical supplies needed restocking. The suction machine needed cleaning after each use. The nasogastric tube required flushing before and after each feed and each medication. He pulled it out twice. Both times in the early hours. He needed repositioning through the night. Every two hours, or close to it. My father's sleep required a particular pillow arrangement. He couldn't say what it was. If it was wrong, a mark appeared on his face by morning. You learned the arrangement by getting it wrong. The knowledge didn't transfer by description. It lived in the correction.

He didn't always know who I was. On the days he did, he was sometimes frightened. On the days he didn't, he was sometimes aggressive. Dementia moves between these without warning and without logic. You learn not to take it personally. You learn this slowly and imperfectly.

I was still billing.

Two counts running at the same time. One was recorded — the firm kept it, clients paid against it. The other had no ledger. No one had built a column for it.

Before all this, I had a life built around contact. A lot of contact. What I didn't understand yet was that contact and knowing are not the same thing. My partner came when he could. He was present. He did not arrive short of trying. But I was always the one going back in.

After my father died, I resigned. Not with a plan. On a feeling.

I arranged for the rented equipment to be collected. The rest I cleared myself. My father's flat sold in eleven weeks. I took less than the agent recommended because I wanted it done. The proceeds sat in an account I didn't look at.

The flat I'd shared with my ex-boyfriend was still partially in my name. We arranged the exit cleanly. He was generous about it. I found this difficult to receive.

I was cash rich in a way I hadn't been before and didn't know what to do with.

I could have gone back to the firm. They would have taken me. I knew this without asking. I also knew, with the same certainty, that I could not go back. Something had grated internally for two years until it could not be ignored. The count had run correctly the whole time. That was exactly the problem.

I had known her for eight years, from a workshop. She offered the room without making it mean more than it was. I moved in for the same reason. The room was quiet. The building was old. The lift was slow. I had spent months in a high-floor unit that smelled of antiseptic and suction fluid, and the slow lift was fine.

I had time I didn't know what to do with.

In the weeks that followed I began to understand what I had been approaching for years. I had been building a life organised around contact. People at the right temperature. Closeness that didn't ask too much. It had felt, while it was working, like a kind of sufficiency. Then the crisis came, and I found out what sufficiency meant in practice: nobody arrived who actually knew me. Nobody had been built for that. I hadn't built for it either.

My father had known me. By the end, he didn't know anyone.

I didn't have the vocabulary for what I was grieving. I knew it included him. I suspected it included other things I was not yet ready to look at directly.

I had helped Mr Tan years earlier with a leasing dispute. More complex than the brief suggested. I did what it required. I did not bill the excess.

I ran into him some months after the resignation. He mentioned a space he wasn't using. On a feeling I asked if I could work in his kitchen instead. Not manage. Just work.

For two months I made coffee and cleared tables. The work had a beginning and an end. After the shift I went back to Lina's and did nothing. The room asked nothing of me either.

In the third month, I started asking him questions. One afternoon I asked what he did with what was left at the end of the week.

He looked at me for a moment. Then he said he didn't have leftovers. He cooked what had been ordered.

I thought about this for several days. Then I started watching more carefully.

Every fortnight he would sit at the back table with a notebook — not long, twenty minutes — and decide what the kitchen would do for the next two weeks. Sometimes he increased. Sometimes he decreased. The venue had high footfall. The capacity to fill more seats was always there. He did not fill it.

If a vegetable was out of season, he took it off. If something had become expensive enough that the quality would suffer, he changed it. He did not find a substitute that held the margin. He changed the dish. Sometimes this meant the menu for a fortnight was smaller than the fortnight before. He cooked what the season and the supply actually gave.

He got the number wrong sometimes. When he got it wrong, he ran short. He never ran over.

If something was out, it was out. No back-pedalling to make things fit.

I had spent two years watching a system produce more output than it could sustain — more shifts, more helpers, more hours billed — because the demand existed and the demand was treated as the instruction. I followed the brief. Mr Tan used the demand as information, not direction. He read the conditions and decided what was possible. Then he cooked to that number and not past it.

He offered me the space eventually. He said: I trust you will turn this into gold. He had no evidence for this. I had not given him any.

I learned in his kitchen what I had been trying to understand for two years. Not recipes. The question of the right number.

The café takes forty orders. The orders come before the cooking — not at the door, not in a queue. By Wednesday the kitchen knows what Saturday holds. If you missed it, you missed it. There is no extra batch.

People ask why I don't expand. The demand is there. Two sittings. Maybe a second location. The formula holds.

The formula is not the point.

The forty is not a constraint on something that wanted to grow. A shoulder of pork is a shoulder of pork. You cook it whole. It yields what it yields. A second shoulder produces more food. It does not produce a better version of the first. The standard drops.

What I understood in my father's flat is that care doesn't scale. Not because the hours aren't there — we hired more people, covered more shifts, the count tracked all of it. The hours could be redistributed. What couldn't be redistributed was the pillow arrangement. You can train the next person in the clinical procedure. You cannot train them in the pillow arrangement except by having them get it wrong and read the mark it leaves. The knowledge lives in the correction. No one else accumulates that correction except the person who keeps showing up. That person was always me. The helpers covered the hours. I was the continuity.

The measuring system I worked in had no column for this. The count was not wrong. It counted what it was built to count.

It just wasn't counting the right thing.
