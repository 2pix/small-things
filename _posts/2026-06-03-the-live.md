At seven fifty-five Lina put the kettle on. While it boiled she switched on the row of lights above her armchair and adjusted the bags on the table — three of her own, a clutch from Nong in Chiang Mai, a small crossbody in several colours — the rose gold hadn't moved in two sales — from another woman whose name she always forgot but whose work she trusted. Phone on the stand, battery pack plugged in. She poured the water over the leaves and left it to steep.

At eight she brought the mug over, settled into the chair, and tapped Go Live.

"Okay," she said to no one and to whoever arrived. "I have this one."

She held up the clutch. Tan, structured, a clasp that clicked properly. She turned it once in her hands.

"The clasp. This is why I trust her." She pressed it open and shut once. The sound was clean. "When a clasp is right you know immediately. When it isn't you spend the whole time worrying about it."

She set it down.

"Admin questions read the link in bio."

Three people in. Then five. She didn't check.

Wen was lying on her side on the sofa.

She had come with the intention of writing. Han Qin had texted at seven: running late, one hour maybe less. She had sat at his desk.

On the pavement that afternoon the phrase had arrived whole: Four hours is more than enough. She had known immediately it was a piece. She opened a document and set her hands on the keys.

What came out was not that.

She wrote one sentence about the driver and it broke into something else — the workshop, the free-writing, fragments she hadn't let herself read back yet, the floor was built so we could stand on it, that was the plan. She stopped. Tried to steer back to the driver. Two more sentences. Then: What was the floor for. It had arrived. She hadn't put it there.

She closed the document. Opened it again. Twenty minutes of this — pulling toward the driver, the thing about stopping before you're wrecked, and some other piece wanting to surface instead. Not fighting her. Just — present. Waiting.

She closed the laptop. Moved to the sofa.

That had been forty minutes ago. She had opened and closed four things since, scrolled past a reel she'd already seen twice, looked at a thread about something she didn't care about enough to remember. The evening had that sticky quality: long, shapeless, not quite restful. Han Qin was working late again. She had told him she didn't mind and she mostly didn't.

She scrolled.

A face stopped her. Not young. Eurasian. A woman somewhere in her seventies, holding a bag up to the camera with the businesslike attention of someone showing a person standing next to them, not an audience. The word LIVE sat in the corner in red.

Wen clicked in.

{% include divider.html %}

"The charity piece goes to the kids in Chiang Mai," Lina said. "Ten percent. Not the whole sale — Nong needs to eat also." She set the clutch down. "Everything else goes to the foundation. I've been doing this three years. The same foundation. They feed kids, that's all."

She picked up her mug and took a sip.

In the comments: A string of hearts, followed by a question about the clasp colour, someone asking about returns.

She answered the clasp question. She did not address returns. There was a note in her bio that covered it. Either they'd read it or they hadn't.

Someone typed a paragraph. A job that was wrong. A relationship that was also wrong. A mother who wanted her to stay. Two years of this, four years of this, the math of staying adding up to something she hadn't agreed to.

She set down her tea.

Typed: @lovetohateme86. You're wearing too many coats.

She did not wait to see the reply. She turned back to the bags.

{% include divider.html %}

Wen watched Lina answer questions with three words or not at all. She watched the clutch sell — someone asked, Lina said the price, the person said yes. No cart function, no link in bio, just a name in the comments and presumably they'd sort the rest. She watched Lina drink her tea. She watched the coat line land in the comments and stay there, nobody replying, whoever had written the paragraph apparently gone quiet.

Wen had felt it before — in the café, in the way the driver had talked, in the afternoon when she'd sat at the workshop and not needed to do anything with her hands.

She typed: How do you tell the truth to someone you love. When the truth is still forming. And you're not sure what it means yet but you know it's real.

She sent it before she could rephrase it.

{% include divider.html %}

Lina saw the question arrive.

She read it once. Twice.

She looked up at the camera.

"Someone is asking about telling the truth." She didn't read the comment out. "When it's not finished yet. When you don't know all the parts."

She picked up the cup and rolled it once in her hands.

"You do it anyway. Because the alternative is you spend the rest of your life watching yourself not do it." She set the cup down. "You think watching is safer. It isn't. Watching just takes longer to arrive at the same place. And you're more tired when you get there."

"That's all."

She picked up the last of her own bags — the structured tote, slate, the one she hadn't posted yet. She turned it once in her hands.

Behind her, the kitchen door opened.

Mark came through. Glass of water. He went to the counter, filled it, went back through. He did not look at the phone. He did not acknowledge the camera.

{% include divider.html %}

Wen saw him.

She sat up slightly.

The man from the café counter. The one who had made the coffee that day she'd sat with her back to the wall and felt her shoulders drop. The one who had moved behind the counter like a person who had decided, once, what that work was for, and then stopped deciding it every day.

She looked at the woman on the screen.

The woman was holding a bag up, talking about the leather on the handles, how long it would last if you didn't leave it in the rain, which most people did but which she chose not to tell them because it didn't change how they'd behave.

Wen looked at the comment she'd sent.

The live had continued. Lina had not waited.

You think watching is safer. It isn't.

She set the phone face-down on the cushion.

She lay back.

She looked at the ceiling.

{% include divider.html %}

Lina closed the live at nine.

No sign-off. No summary of what had sold or what had been said. She pressed the button and the thing ended. She picked up the bags and put them back in their place by the wall. Three of hers, Nong's clutch already sold, the rose gold crossbody still there.

She clicked off the light above the armchair. Then the kitchen.

In the morning she would check if the woman had come back. If the question held up, she would offer a reading.
