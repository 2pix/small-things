I stayed.

It didn't feel like a dungeon.

It felt clean. Well-lit. Built from good intentions and language so soft it barely left marks.

That was the first lie.

People walked in and out easily. Laughed over coffee. Changed their minds about dinner. Followed impulses that arrived without apology.

He stayed.

Not because the door was locked. Because staying felt like the shape his body had already learned.

The bars were thin. Polished. Almost elegant in the afternoon light. They had names that sounded like virtues—niceness, reliability, steady. Being the one who could carry it. He built them carefully, the way you'd build anything you believed would keep the people you loved from feeling the weight shift unexpectedly.

Every time he swallowed a no, another bar slid into place. Chi-chik. Precise. Barely enough sound to mark the moment.

Every time he absorbed someone else's disappointment so their day could continue smoothly, the walls rose a fraction higher. Barely noticeable. Just enough.

Every time someone relaxed because he didn't need anything, the dungeon gained another lock.

"You're so understanding."

"You're so calm."

"You're so easy."

The words land warm. They keep landing.

You don't hear the sound the bars make when they settle. That small click of alignment. Metal finding its groove.

Inside, it's quiet. Predictable. The light is good. Everyone else moves freely—needs things, changes plans mid-sentence, follows desire wherever it leads them. He stays steady so no one has to feel the architecture shift beneath them.

For a long time, nothing hurt. There were no bruises darkening on his ribs. No threats. No visible damage. Just a low, constant pressure to keep holding everything together. To be the place where weight could land without consequence.

The moment it changed wasn't loud.

A small choice was made elsewhere. A familiar weight landed where it always did—on his shoulders, in his schedule, somewhere in the space he'd learned to keep available. And suddenly he realized his hands weren't just resting on the bars anymore.

He was holding them up.

Not gripping them in desperation. Not rattling them in protest. Just keeping them from falling. Keeping the structure standing. His shoulders bearing the load so quietly he'd stopped noticing the ache.

The dungeon wasn't holding him.

He was the structure.

That's when the air changed.

Letting go wasn't dramatic. He didn't announce it at dinner. Didn't rage. Didn't sit anyone down to explain how the bars had been built with his own careful hands, one swallowed need at a time. He didn't accuse anyone of building this place with him, though they'd certainly walked through it comfortably enough.

He just stopped.

Stopped catching weight before it landed. Stopped adjusting his shape so everyone else could stay relaxed. Stopped performing the easy steadiness that had made him so reliable, so calm, so understanding.

The first bar he released shook the room.

Not because it was strong—because everything else depended on it staying exactly where he'd put it.

People noticed immediately.

"You seem different."

"Are you okay?"

"What's going on with you?"

The questions came with a small edge of concern. Or was it discomfort? Hard to tell. Both, probably.

He didn't explain.

When he stepped back, the dungeon didn't collapse the way he'd feared it might. It revealed itself.

One foot. Then the other. His body remembering how to move as he walked, without checking first if anyone needed him to stay still.

The door was never locked.

It had never even been closed.
