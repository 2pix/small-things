This is part of The Black Cat — a novel in pieces.

She arrived at the Black Cat at ten past nine with a bag of galangal she hadn't trusted the assistant to buy correctly. And the brioche loaves — Mark's, from the morning, taken from the oven at seven forty-five.

The café was already open. Mark was at the counter with his back to the room, adjusting something on the machine. He heard her come in — she knew this from the slight change in his posture, nothing more.

She set them on his side of the counter.

"The forty today," she said.

He reached for something — the wrong thing, already ahead of himself. She shook her head once. He put it back. He laughed, which he was allowed to do. Her hand on his shoulder briefly as she passed.

That was the arrangement. His side ran by his logic. Hers ran by hers.

<p class="divider">❖</p>

The kitchen assistant was already in — she had a key and had learned to start the rice at eight-forty-five without being told. Forty portions. The cooker was running.

"Pork?" Lina said.

"Still going." She nodded at the pot on the back burner. "I added the mushrooms at eight like you said."

Lina lifted the lid. The smell came up — taucheo, dark soy, ginger, the particular low sweetness of pork that had been going since the night before. She had braised the shoulder herself yesterday afternoon, cooled it, refrigerated it overnight. This morning's two hours were the finishing. The fat would have settled and been skimmed. The meat would be at the stage where it held together and gave when asked.

She tasted the liquid.

Adjusted nothing. Put the lid back.

"The galangal," she said, setting the bag on the counter. "Slice it thin. Add it now."

<p class="divider">❖</p>

Forty sets confirmed — she had checked the list yesterday evening, her eyes running down the names. She had told the assistant what to prep. Some names she recognised and a few she didn't, which meant someone had shared the number.

She didn't take walk-ins. There was simply nothing to buy if you arrived without an order. This had confused people once or twice. They had looked at the chalkboard above the counter where Mark wrote the day's coffee offerings and found no food menu. She had watched this happen from the kitchen doorway.

The system was not designed to be understood from the outside. It was designed to feed forty people a good lunch. That was all it needed to do.

She cooked more than forty. She always had. Food didn't arrive at exact numbers — a shoulder of pork was a shoulder of pork, and she cooked it whole, not to a count. What was left over after the orders were filled would find its way somewhere. Mark would eat. The assistant would eat. A regular who came in hoping might be lucky. Her food was always eaten. She had never once thrown any out.

<p class="divider">❖</p>

The vegetables were kai lan — cleaned, blanched, ready to go in the wok close to noon. She had her own method: high heat, garlic, a small amount of oyster sauce at the end, not before. The timing mattered. Kai lan could sit for two minutes without loss. Three was too many.

The assistant knew this. Lina had told her once, in the first week. She had not told her again.

She moved through the prep the way she moved through the morning — without hurrying, without pausing. Portioning the rice. Checking the containers. The pork to be sliced at eleven-thirty, not before, so it didn't dry at the edges. The sauce to be tasted once more at eleven. The galangal in now.

The kitchen held its particular sounds: the rice cooker's hiss, the low bubble from the back burner, the knife on the board. Through the pass-through she could hear the café — the machine, the occasional voice, the door. Mark's domain doing its own work.

<p class="divider">❖</p>

At eleven-fifteen she tasted the sauce again.

This time she added a small amount of palm sugar. Not much. Enough to round something that had gone slightly sharp. She replaced the lid.

The assistant was watching.

"How do you know?" she said.

Lina looked at her.

"Every time. How do you know what it needs?"

"You taste it," Lina said. "Then you wait. Then you taste it again."

She considered this as if it were an answer to a different question. Which it was.

<p class="divider">❖</p>

The first orders came at twelve-ten.

Lina stayed in the kitchen. The assistant handled the pass — containers out, names checked against the list, nothing hurried, nothing announced. People came in, gave their name, received their order, found a table or left with it.

She watched through the pass-through, occasionally, without appearing to watch.

The pork was right. She knew from the first set — the way she laid it out, the sauce sitting properly, the kai lan still holding colour. She had made this dish close to the way her mother had made it and not close at all, which was the right relationship to an inherited recipe.

By one-fifteen the forty sets were gone.

The kitchen was quiet.

The assistant was already cleaning. Lina wiped down the counter herself.

<p class="divider">❖</p>

She passed through the café on her way out.

Three tables still occupied — the particular quality of a Saturday afternoon beginning. Mark was at the counter. She stopped beside him. He poured her a small cup without being asked — the end-of-service coffee she didn't ask for and didn't refuse.

She drank it standing.

"Full house," he said. He meant the forty.

"Full house," she said.

She set the cup down. Picked up her bag.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"Sunday."

She didn't do Sundays. She had the live sale tonight — the bags, the TikTok, people asking questions in the comments she would answer in three words or not at all. She needed the afternoon for herself first. He knew all of this.

She left.
