She sat in the car for a few minutes before going up.

The engine had been off for a while — she wasn't sure how long. She could hear it cooling: small sounds, metal settling back into itself after the heat. The carpark had its own quiet. Fluorescent lights, the same colour as nothing. She was still in the posture of driving — hands in her lap now, shoulders not quite dropped. Other people's grief still on her clothes somewhere, the particular weight of a room full of condolence.

She was just listening. To the car. To the level of quiet that follows an engine. To whatever it was she'd brought out with her from the afternoon.

She had David's number now.

She looked at the phone on the passenger seat. There was a feeling she didn't have words for — she noticed it the way you notice a shift in air pressure, something arriving before the mind catches up. Not quite warmth. Not quite weight. Something that had been at a particular angle for a long time, and was now, very slightly, at a different one.

She picked up her phone and put it in her bag. Then she went up.

Her mother had left the gate unlocked. She knew before she turned the handle.

The flat smelled the same it always had — the particular combination of her mother's cooking, her father's liniment, the old wood of the cabinet by the door. She had grown up in this smell. She had also spent thirteen years not living in it, which meant she now noticed it.

Yogi heard her before she reached the door.

She opened it and then the jump — his whole body wiggling, the greeting he kept only for her. She knew this. Her parents had mentioned it once, matter-of-factly: he doesn't do that for us. She had not said anything to that.

One minute. Maybe less. Then done — he dropped back to four feet on the floor, shook himself once, and stood at his usual distance. An arm's length, maybe more.

He watched her.

She put her bag down. Didn't move toward him.

He looked at her face. Then her posture. Slowly, without urgency. The particular attention of an animal who'd learned that being useful required accuracy first.

Then he came toward her.

Not rushing — just closing the distance. When he reached her he stepped on her foot with his front paw. She bent down to pick him up. He placed his chin on her shoulder and stayed. His whole weight in it. Twelve years old, muzzle going grey, breathing slower than it used to be.

She held him — just staying in it. The top of his head smelled like fresh laundry, mixed in with the cooking smells of home caught in his curls. She thought about the years she'd had him, which included the apartment and the marriage and the particular Tuesday she'd driven to the shelter telling herself she was just looking.

She had been looking. Just not only at dogs.

Yogi was the one system she'd been allowed to change. Not her own diet, not her parents', not anything in the hospital canteen. His. She had run her upstream experiments on him for years — herbs, timing, what she'd found in the research she didn't mention at dinner. He had been the channel. He was still the channel.

"Okay," she whispered. "I'm here."

Her mother appeared from the kitchen.

"Eat already?"

"At the reception."

"That kind of food." A sound that meant: I'll make something.

"Ma. I'm fine."

Her mother looked at her the way she had always looked at her — reading something, not saying what she read. Then she went back to the kitchen.

Her father was in the living room. The news on. He raised one hand when she came through.

"Funeral okay?"

"Yes."

"Sad thing." He meant the person who had died, not whatever was on her face. Or maybe both. Her father had always been efficient with condolence.

She sat in the chair adjacent to the sofa. Yogi settled near her feet.

This was the life she'd come back to: her parents' flat in Bishan, one room that used to be hers and was now a room she slept in. The reduced version. Laptop, clothes, the essentials. A dog who was more hers than anything she had left behind.

There hadn't been much to leave behind. That was the thing she was still understanding.

She had thought — not recently, but at some point during the marriage — that she had built something. The job. The apartment. The life that matched his life. His hours, his colleagues, his vocabulary for what was important. She had climbed because it was the available direction and because standing still felt like a choice nobody was allowed to make. She had learned to talk about outcomes and KPIs and staff retention and she had learned to read a meeting before it started and she had become, somewhere in the course of all that, someone who was good at a thing she had not wanted.

Her ex-husband had not been unkind. That was the hardest part to explain. He had been present and logical and had not entertained her research — not because he was cruel but because it didn't fit the way he organised knowledge. He was a clinician. Evidence, intervention, protocol. She had kept a folder on her laptop she didn't mention at dinner. Cholesterol. Blood pressure. Blood sugar. The research that said these were not the problem — they were the body's accounting of something running upstream, compensating for years before any number showed. The intervention treated the readout. Nobody was asking what produced it. She had walked Yogi at 7am and 8pm and in the walking done the thinking she couldn't do anywhere else.

She had not noticed when the walks became the only time the rest of it felt real — the sixty minutes of quiet that made the weight of everything else visible by contrast.

The marriage ended the way a lot of things ended: not with a large event but with the slow settling of a borrowed coat — put on when the weather required it, worn so long the shape of it had become her own. There had been the children question — not a fight, a conversation that happened once and then didn't happen again. There had been the folder on the laptop. There had been thirteen years and the correct apartment and the dog she'd got because she'd needed something to come home to that was purely glad.

She thought about what David said. In the carpark. Not the first things but the thing he said at the end, when the evening was already over and she was already standing with her keys.

He had not explained or justified. He had named what he had done and why, at twenty-five, he had not been able to stay inside something that felt that real. Not as a bid for forgiveness. Just as the plain thing, said to the person it belonged to. Nothing else. No other move.

Nobody had said an accurate thing to her in a long time.

She sat with it. Her father's news programme. Yogi breathing at her feet. Her mother in the kitchen making something she didn't need.

It didn't break her.

It opened something. Something that had been sitting closed for twenty years — not sealed, not painful, just closed long enough that it had stopped trying to open.

She was not going to call him tonight. She was not going to do anything tonight. But she noticed that the window was open and the air coming through it was familiar in a way she had not been prepared for.

Yogi shifted. Put his chin on her foot.

"I know," she said, to him or to herself.

Her mother came in with a bowl of porridge. Set it on the side table without asking.

Amandha looked at it for a moment. Then she ate.

She ate slowly. Her father's programme changed to a documentary she recognised — the blue zones, the researchers who had gone to Sardinia and Okinawa to study why people there regularly lived past a hundred, eating from their gardens, walking, knowing their neighbours. He made the sound he always made when something met with mild approval and settled back into the sofa. Her mother had arranged herself in the armchair closest to the television — the one she always chose when Amandha was home, the small unconscious adjustment of someone who had been making room for a long time.

She thought about James. Not David but James. Present, logical, not-unkind James, who had wanted children and eventually gotten a divorce and was probably, by now, making someone else's life match his.

The children conversation had happened once. She'd felt the relief of that. She hadn't examined the relief.

Her mother moved in the armchair — the small, practised accommodation, a body that had learned exactly how much space it could take.

His name on the school forms. Her face at the school gate. His salary, her calendar. She'd known what the trajectory of it from the first year — had felt it without having words for it. And she'd said no to the thing her body was showing her without being able to say why.

It wasn't that she hadn't wanted children.

She sat with that.

Somewhere in the flat, a clock ticked. She had lived in this room for years before she left and had never once heard it.

What she'd wanted — she held this carefully, watching her mother reset the cushion, the small adjustment of a woman who had always managed her own comfort without making it a question — was someone who would know which day had been hard. Not as help. Both of them in the actual project, shoulder to shoulder, facing the same direction.

So she'd said no to children. Which was technically accurate.

Her mother said, from the armchair: "You want more?"

"No, Ma. I'm fine."

Her mother looked at the bowl. Then at her. Then back at the television.

She watched her father not look up from the screen. Her mother not ask to be asked.

Her father had not asked if her mother wanted tea when he made his. She had noticed this at five. At thirty-two. She noticed it again now.

Her mother would not have wanted him to ask. She'd have said I'll get it and gotten it, because getting it herself was faster and she knew how she liked it. She had internalised the arrangement so completely that it was invisible — to both of them, probably. Independence and self-sufficiency as identical. Neither of them looking at what the arrangement actually was.

The clock, from wherever it was.

Amandha had married a man who would not ask either.

The same sound.

She had told herself this was fine. She was competent. She knew how she liked things. She didn't need to be asked. She'd been right about that. She'd needed something else. Someone for whom the question was worth raising.

They had built what was available to them. Her mother had made a life inside the deal and made it clean and made it hers — the kitchen as a precise argument for her own competence. Her father was a good man. The deal was not a crime. It was what a life looked like. For a very long time.

She had thought she'd done something different.

She hadn't. Her career instead of her mother's domestics. His name on things. Her energy inside them. The deal, updated for a generation that didn't call it the deal anymore.

Yogi shifted against her ankle. He was getting old — she could feel it in the careful way he found his position. He had been in the apartment and in the marriage and in the afternoons when she drove around before going home.

She had known, on those drives. Not the words — not the precise articulation of what she was holding right now. But the sense that something in the structure was wrong and that she was the one carrying the wrongness. Without being able to put it down.

So she'd walked the dog instead.

She'd walked the dog, and made the porridge nobody asked for, and said no to children, and been quietly, accurately wrong about the reasons.

Her mother came back for the bowl without asking. Her father didn't look up from the screen. A normal evening. The exact texture of a family that had been a family long enough to run on low maintenance.

Amandha sat for another moment.

The window David had opened — she was not going to close it. Not because she had a plan. Because for the first time in a long time she could see plainly what was on the other side all along.

It was just what was actually there.

She reached down and put her hand on Yogi's back. He didn't stir. Just breathed, warm and slow, his grey muzzle on her foot.
