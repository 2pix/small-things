The lift always took a while in that block.

She'd pressed the button and was running a thumb along the spine of her notebook when she heard footsteps and turned.

Amandha. Su Lin's daughter.

The marriage. The divorce, or something close enough to a divorce that her mother narrated it the same way. The dog. The flat she'd heard about most recently — tenants just moved out, Amandha moving in.

She wondered if Amandha had a version of her too. A composite assembled from what Su Lin passed on. The job, the boyfriend, the writing she'd been doing that her mother described variously as a blog, a newsletter. She thought: probably yes.

They got in. Three mirrored walls gave them back to themselves.

"Writing workshop," Wen said when Amandha asked. And then: "it's on Tuesdays. I've been going a few months."

Amandha said "writing" the way people say it when they're trying the word out.

Wen could feel the warmth beginning in her neck before she'd finished the sentence. She thought of the way Kaia had looked up at her when she entered.

She touched the back of her neck briefly, without meaning to, before she dropped her hand.

"It's been good," she said.

Amandha looked at her with the expression of someone who has learned to carry things without it showing. Found her a little earnest. A little still-on-the-original-plan. She smiled.

"Can I get your number?" Wen said.

Amandha blinked — slight surprise, then: "sure." She gave it. Wen put it in, typed hello, sent.

Amandha's phone buzzed. She looked at it, then looked up with something close to amusement.

"I already have yours," she said.

Wen looked at her.

"Your mother," Amandha said.

"Right," Wen said.

"Mine too," Amandha said. And then: "we should have coffee. Properly. Not just the lift."

The lift opened at the ground floor. Wen went right — toward the bus stop, the workshop, Tuesday. Amandha went left.

Her face was still warm when she got to the bus stop. She stood at the kerb and touched her phone in her pocket. In her messages: Amandha, a contact that had apparently existed for some time. Hello.

The mothers. Of course.

By the tenth Tuesday, she couldn't have explained this to someone who hadn't been there from the beginning — the chairs hadn't changed, the fluorescent light in the far corner still buzzed until the facilitator turned it off, people arrived in the same order. But four hours is a long time to be in a room with the same people reading what they actually wrote instead of what they meant to write.

The composure was expensive. After a while people stopped paying for it. Not all of them, not all at once — there were still the pieces where you could feel someone explaining themselves before the work had time to explain itself. But there were moments now, usually in the third hour, when something was read and nobody moved to fill it.

Kaia was already there.

Notebook open, pen in hand, looking at the wall — not at the door when Wen came in, not at the chairs filling around her. Inside something already. The kind of stillness that didn't need the room to know it was there.

Wen took the seat across and a little left. She put her bag under the chair. She arranged her notebook on the table. She took out her phone and typed: workshop runs late tonight, going to be close to eleven. She didn't look up while she typed it. She put the phone away.

She did not look over at Kaia.

{% include divider.html %}

The facilitator set the timer. Sixty minutes, whatever is alive, you don't have to know what it is before you start.

For twenty minutes Wen wrote around the thing she was trying to write. She knew this was happening. She kept going anyway.

She wrote about the MRT in the mornings, the carriage full, standing with the Kindle four inches from her face. She had bought the book herself — two weeks ago, lying on her side of the bed, Han Qin's breathing beside her, regular and slightly loud. She'd had the screen brightness all the way down. She had put the phone face-down on the nightstand and lain looking at the ceiling. She had not mentioned the book to Han Qin. She had not thought about why she had not mentioned it.

She crossed that out.

She wrote about a woman she'd had coffee with on Thursday. A friend of a friend — someone who had spent two years freelancing after leaving her job, wanting to own her time, wanting to stop answering to someone else's calendar. She had worked herself to the bone and it had not felt like freedom; it had felt like the same constraint wearing different clothes. So she had gone back to working for someone. But the someone was paying three thousand a month, and at three thousand there was no breathing room — every unexpected thing a calculation, the body running the numbers constantly. She'd said: "I thought going back would be easier. I didn't expect it to be harder in a completely different way." She had touched her coffee cup while she said it, turned it once in her hands without lifting it. She was exhausted and she didn't know which direction to be exhausted toward.

Wen had thought about her on the bus home. She was still thinking about her now.

She wrote: there is a specific kind of tiredness that comes from trying every available route and finding each one depleted in a different way. It is not the tiredness of failure. It is the tiredness of effort correctly applied to the wrong question.

She stopped. Pressed her fingertips to the page without meaning to.

She kept going.

The floor was built so we could stand on it. I have been standing on it for a long time. I am only recently asking what the standing is for.

Below it, after a space:

I have been carrying something for several weeks that doesn't have a name yet. It arrived before I noticed it arriving. I have not been looking at it directly because looking at it directly would require me to know what it was.

I think I know what it is.

I have been thinking about what to do with the knowing. This is the wrong question.

She crossed out the last line. Left the rest.

{% include divider.html %}

People read. A man who had written about his father's hands — not what they built, just the hands themselves, the way he'd memorised them knuckle by knuckle without knowing he was memorising. A woman who had brought the same piece three weeks in a row and each time it was shorter and this time it was four sentences and it was right. Someone whose piece didn't work and they knew it as they read and they kept going anyway, their pen turning slowly in their fingers while they spoke, and that mattered more than the piece.

The facilitator said: anyone else before we break?

Kaia said: I'll read.

She had a short piece. She said before she read it: "this one I don't know what it is yet. I'm reading it to find out."

She read about a corridor. A hospital corridor at the shift change, the particular two minutes when both shifts are present and the space is briefly double-occupied. She wrote about the handover. The information passed from one set of hands to another. She wrote: what doesn't transfer is not in the notes. It transfers anyway. I watch it happen. I have not found the word for it.

There is a woman from admin who was sent to watch me. I know this. She stayed three nights past the point the job required. At the shift change she still stops. She still watches. I don't know what she is looking for now. I'm not sure I'm looking away.

Last week she offered me a direction. I have not said yes. I have not said no. I am still trying to find out if she is offering it because she sees what I do, or because it is easier to move me than to explain me.

She stopped. She looked at the page a moment longer, her thumb resting against the edge of the notebook.

"I've been trying to write that for a year," she said. "That's all I've got."

The room held what it was holding. A woman across the table pressed her fingers together. Someone at the far end had gone very still.

{% include divider.html %}

Second block. Another hour.

Wen wrote about a woman who had just left a job she'd held for eleven years. Not fired. Just walked through a glass door for the last time and couldn't explain why. "My parents built their whole lives so I wouldn't have to start from zero," she'd said. "And now I'm —" She'd stopped. Turned her cup in her hands. "Now I'm throwing it away."

She wasn't throwing it away. But Wen understood why it felt that way.

She wrote: there is a specific weight that comes with having been given everything.

She wrote: the survival was not the whole story. It was the floor. The floor was built to stand on — not to stay on, to stand on, to start from.

She wrote about the woman from Thursday, her coffee cup turning in her hands, trying every available route and finding each one exhausted. She wrote the Thursday woman alongside the eleven-year woman alongside the floor sentence and didn't try to make them connect.

{% include divider.html %}

The fourth hour.

People had stopped composing themselves.

The man at the far end had taken off his glasses and set them beside his notebook.

The facilitator went around the circle. Wen read what she'd kept: the Thursday woman, the floor sentence, the knowing without a name.

She didn't explain how they connected. She read them in the order she'd written them.

Silence.

Not the polite kind.

Somewhere at the table, a pen clicked.

"The last one does something to the first," someone said. "The third one especially. You're not done with that."

Wen nodded. Her hand was flat on the page.

Kaia didn't say anything during feedback. She was looking at the table, her thumb moving once along the rim of her pen. When Wen glanced up at the end she wasn't looking at the table anymore.

{% include divider.html %}

In the corridor afterward, the group thinning, people heading toward the lift, the bus stop, the Tuesday night that had finally released them.

Wen said: do you want to get coffee?

Kaia looked at her. "Sure."

{% include divider.html %}

The place near the interchange was still open. Yellow overhead light. A few other tables, no one they knew.

Kaia ordered kopi-o. Wen ordered the same.

They talked about the writing. The corridor piece — Kaia said she'd been carrying it for months, kept adding to it, each addition explained something that had been working without the explanation. "I kept making it worse," she said. "I finally just stopped." She turned her cup in her hands, considering.

Wen said: "that's what I do. I write around it until I run out of room."

"Does it work?"

Wen thought about the pieces she'd read in the fourth hour. "Sometimes the running out is the point."

Wen said: "the second part. The woman from admin."

Kaia turned her cup once. "Someone who was sent to review my file. She stayed longer than the job required."

"What did she offer you?"

"A direction." She set the cup down and let out a slow breath. "I haven't decided if it's for me or for the institution."

"Can you tell?"

"Not yet."

They talked about the man with his father's hands. The woman's piece getting shorter each week. Kaia had noticed what Wen had noticed — that the fourth version was the one that had stopped explaining itself and become the thing itself.

The kopi was too sweet. Wen wrapped both hands around the glass and drank it anyway.

There was a point — she marked it later, tried to locate it in the conversation, couldn't — where she understood she was going to say it. Not because it was the right moment. Because saying it had become less effort than not saying it.

She turned the cup a quarter turn.

She said: "I think I like you."

She said it to the glass.

Kaia didn't move. Not toward. Not away. She stayed exactly where she was — elbows on the table, both hands around her own cup.

She said: "I wondered."

Wen looked up.

Kaia said: "I wasn't sure if you knew." She touched her thumb to the handle of the cup, once. "I'm glad you said it."

The light was the same overhead. The table was the same.

Wen said: "I don't know what to do with it."

She said it to the glass, not to Kaia.

Kaia waited. When Wen looked up, she said: "you don't have to do anything with it right now."

They talked for another fifteen minutes. A book Kaia was reading. Something that had happened at the hospital that week. The facilitator's note in the second block about compression.

{% include divider.html %}

She walked to the bus stop alone.

The estate was quiet in the way it got after eleven — not empty, just settled. She walked through it.

On the bus she checked her phone. Han Qin had replied ok forty-three minutes ago. She looked at it. She held the phone in both hands for a moment. Then she put it in her bag.

She leaned her head against the glass and watched the lit-up estate slide by.

The feeling was still there — not smaller for having been said.
