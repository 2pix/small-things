He got home at seven-thirty.

The apartment was exactly as he'd left it. This was always true and had never bothered him before — the particular stillness of a space that held its shape without him, that didn't need tending, that was simply there when he returned and simply not-there when he didn't. He had chosen it for this. The efficiency of it. The clean lines. The view from the living room window that gave him sky and the middle distance of the city without requiring him to be part of either.

He stood in the doorway for a moment.

The air had the quality of air that hadn't moved all day. Not stale exactly. Just — still. The kind of still that accumulates in a space that has been empty since morning and is waiting for something that hasn't arrived yet to make it a room again rather than just a collection of surfaces and objects.

He put his keys down.

The loan repayment was automatic. He didn't think about it most months — the number leaving his account on the fifteenth with the quiet efficiency of a system he'd set up years ago and stopped noticing. Tonight he thought about it. The car in the carpark below doing the same thing. The two of them running in the background like a hum he'd learned to hear as silence.

He looked at the living room.

The couch he'd chosen carefully. The shelves with the books he'd meant to read. The print on the wall he'd bought at a gallery three years ago because it was good and he'd been in the right mood and the price had been reasonable. All of it chosen. All of it his. All of it sitting in the still air with the patience of things that don't need you to validate their existence.

He freshened up. Changed his shirt. Got back in the car.

His mother had made chap chye.

He could smell it from the entrance — the long-cooked softness of it, the lily buds and black fungus and glass noodles that had been going since the afternoon, the smell of a dish that required patience and rewarded it. The babi pong tay was already on the table, dark and glossy, the fermented beans giving it that particular depth that you couldn't manufacture quickly, that needed time to become what it was.

Marina had outdone herself or his mother had directed differently tonight or the combination of the two had produced something that arrived in his body before he sat down.

He was hungry.

He hadn't known that until the smell reached him.

His father was already at the table with the newspaper. His mother came out of the kitchen with the chap chye, set it down, looked at him.

"You look better," she said.

"You said that last week," he said.

"Last week you didn't," she said. "Tonight you do."

He sat down.

She served him. The chap chye first, generous, the way she served things she knew he liked. Then the babi pong tay, the sauce dark and deep, a piece of pork belly that had been in the pot long enough to give without resistance.

He ate.

It tasted like itself. Like the particular combination of his mother's direction and Marina's hands and years of the same dish made in the same kitchen with the same patience. He ate without performing enjoyment, without narrating it, without doing anything except being a body that was hungry and being fed.

His father folded the newspaper.

They talked about New York. His mother had been on the phone with Alina again — the apartment was almost ready, Nathan had found a good removalist, there was apparently an excellent Peranakan restaurant three blocks away which his mother found both pleasing and suspicious. His father had looked at flights. Direct was better. Business class if they were going for two weeks, he wasn't arriving stiff.

David listened. Asked about Nathan's family. His mother brightened — she'd spoken to Nathan's mother once, briefly, a warm woman, they'd agreed to meet properly when his parents visited.

He ate the babi pong tay.

Something in him was looser than it had been all week. Looser than the cardiologist's office. Looser than the studio, the monitor, the clean results. Just — here. At this table. Eating this food. His parents' voices doing what they did, the particular music of two people who had been talking to each other for decades and had stopped needing to perform the talking.

His father was saying something about the hotel. Whether to stay near Alina or in Midtown.

David looked at the babi pong tay.

"I'll check on the house while you're gone," he said. "And Marina."

His father looked up. "Good. Three weeks. We leave the fourteenth."

He reached into his pocket. Put the spare key on the table between them.

David looked at it.

His father's hand still near it. The key sitting on the tablecloth between the chap chye and the babi pong tay, ordinary and specific, the particular key he'd seen on his father's ring his whole life.

He picked it up.

It was warm from his father's pocket.

"I'd like to stay," he said. "Sometimes. While you're gone."

The words came out before he'd decided to say them.

His father went still for just a moment. The CFO reading something that wasn't on a balance sheet. Filing it. Not asking.

"The room is there," his father said.

His mother said nothing. Served him more chap chye.

The key sat in David's palm.

He closed his hand around it.

He got home at nine-fifteen.

The apartment received him the way it always did. Still. Unchanged. The air that hadn't moved.

He opened his laptop. Found the flights to Chiang Mai. There was one on Sunday morning, early, a connection through Bangkok, arriving late afternoon. Reasonable price. He booked it without deliberating.

Closed the laptop.

Sat on the floor.

Not the way he'd sat on the studio floor — not from the weight of something that had stopped holding. Just — down. Cross-legged, his back against the couch, the apartment dark except for the city light coming through the window, the hum of the loan and the car and the air conditioning that kept the still air at a temperature nobody had chosen for tonight specifically.

He sat in the dark.

He imagined the studio first.

He let it assemble in his mind. The long table. The light in the morning. Priya and James. The mockups. The sound of the place when it was running well, the particular productive hum of people doing good work. His desk. The laptop. The proposals. The width of it, the responsibility of it, the satisfaction of having built something that worked.

He noticed what his chest did.

Sat with it.

Then the apartment.

This room. This floor. The couch behind him. The print on the wall he couldn't see in the dark but knew was there. The loan running on the fifteenth. The car below. The still air. The quiet that had been here when he left this morning and would be here when he woke tomorrow and the morning after.

He noticed what his chest did.

Different.

Then his parents' house.

The chap chye smell still faint on his clothes. Marina's program through the door. The key in his pocket — he could feel it now, the specific weight and shape of it, warm still or just familiar. His father at the table with the newspaper. His mother serving him without asking. The particular music of two people who had stopped needing to perform the talking.

He noticed what his chest did.

He sat with all three.

He didn't try to interpret. Didn't open the laptop to look anything up. Didn't reach for the forums or the biohacking threads or the clean language of men who had solved the problem of the body's inconvenient limits.

Just sat.

The apartment was quiet around him.

Not the quiet of the tea house in Guangzhou. Not the quiet of the river going gold. Not even the quiet of his parents' house after dinner, the particular inhabited quiet of a space that held people even when the people weren't in it.

This was a different quiet.

The quiet of a space that had stopped waiting.

He sat in it for a long time.

The key in his pocket.

Chiang Mai on Sunday.

Something in his chest that he didn't have a name for yet, that the cardiologist's monitor hadn't caught, that Google didn't have a forum for, that was simply — information. His body offering it quietly in the dark, the way his body had been offering it for months, waiting for him to stop being busy enough to receive it.
